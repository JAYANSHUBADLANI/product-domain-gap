## 2026-09-08, first real results

The archive finished at 6,650,336,447 bytes, matching the size recorded in `protocol.yaml` exactly, and its sha256 matched the recorded one too, `93378c8d...fd67`, so the download is the exact archive the protocol was locked against, not a corrupted or partial one that happened to be the right size.

Extraction pulled exactly 4,000 files, 20 categories times 100 images times 2 domains, every (domain, category) pair at its expected count, zero exact-duplicate groups anywhere in the extracted set. The duplicate-atomic-unit logic in `splits.py` never had anything to actually merge on real data, which is worth stating plainly since the tests for it only exercised a fabricated duplicate group, not a real one, so this run doesn't confirm that logic against real duplicate images, only that the real data happened not to need it. Splits landed at their exact target sizes for every category, 70/10/20 for Product and 50/50 for Real Life, with no atomic-unit deviation for the same reason.

Trained the classifier head: 15 epochs, best validation accuracy 0.880, 234.6 seconds on `mps`. Locked checkpoint sha256 `80f453911abe2da49b20e8b3df3a1f649cd37f8943b685e4e72816bfda98ab1`.

The headline result: class-balanced top-1 accuracy 0.8275 on the clean Product test split (95% CI 0.7925 to 0.86, 2000 bootstrap resamples), 0.482 on the shifted Real Life test split (95% CI 0.453 to 0.512). Gap: 0.3455. The interval on the shifted number doesn't come close to the clean number's interval, so this isn't a claim resting on a single point estimate that could plausibly have landed the other way; the central claim from the protocol lock holds, clearly, on this run.

Per-category accuracy on the shifted split isn't evenly distributed around that 0.482 mean, it's bimodal: `computer mouse` 0.80, `keyboard` 0.74, `game controller` 0.68 hold up close to or above the overall clean number, while `usb stick` 0.04, `network switch` 0.06, and `computer` 0.14 collapse almost entirely. I haven't dug into why beyond a hypothesis: the categories that survive are the ones whose whole-object shape stays recognizable regardless of scale, angle, or what else is in frame, a mouse or a controller usually appears as itself, close to full frame, in either domain, while a usb stick or a network switch in a real customer photo is often small, partially occluded, or shown as one component in a larger scene the studio photograph never has to contend with. That's a hypothesis from category names and general knowledge of what these objects look like in casual photos, not something confirmed yet by actually looking at the shifted-domain images themselves, which the saliency phase and a closer per-category image review should speak to more directly.

The confidence-gap analysis says the model is somewhat, not dramatically, self-aware about its shifted-domain mistakes: mean confidence 0.695 on correct shifted predictions against 0.478 on incorrect ones. 0.478 is well above the 0.05 a uniform guess across 20 classes would carry, so a meaningful share of the shifted misses are the model being confidently wrong, not uncertain and wrong, which matters for what a downstream system built on this classifier could safely assume about its own low-confidence outputs versus its wrong ones.

Recovery curve is running now, the slow phase, 8 budgets times 3 nested-sampling runs, each restarting from the locked clean checkpoint. No per-cycle progress printing exists in `recovery.py` or `run_pipeline.py` yet, an oversight I noticed only once the phase was already running and decided not to interrupt for; checking process health during the wait with `ps` CPU percentage rather than assuming from a silent log, the same lesson I'd already learned the hard way on a different project's evaluation stalls.

## 2026-09-08, recovery curve, a mid-run correctness fix, and saliency

Stopped to ask myself whether the recovery sweep could be safely paused partway through. It couldn't, as written: `run_recovery_curve` only wrote anything to disk after all 24 cycles finished, so killing it at cycle 20 would have lost cycles 1 through 20 as completely as cycle 1. Fixed before continuing rather than after a stall forced the question: `run_recovery_curve` now takes a `progress_path`, appends one line of JSON to it immediately after each cycle completes (flushed, not buffered), and `load_completed_cycles` skips any (run, labels_per_class) pair already present in that file on the next call, so a killed run resumes at the next uncompleted cycle instead of redoing finished work. Killed the then-running sweep (about 13 minutes in, nothing lost that mattered since nothing had been saved anywhere yet) and restarted with the fix. Added per-cycle print output at the same time, which the first attempt also lacked, so progress is now visible live rather than only inferred from `ps` CPU percentage.

The restarted sweep finished in 1275.6 seconds, about 21 minutes, faster than the roughly 50 to 70 minutes I'd estimated going in:

The honest shape of this curve isn't a clean, monotonically flattening recovery curve, and I'm reporting it as such rather than smoothing over what it actually did. From 0 to 8 labelled examples per category, the climb is steep and consistent across all 3 runs, 0.482 to 0.642, recovering close to half of the 0.3455 clean-to-shifted gap with a genuinely small amount of labelled data. Past 8, the curve stops behaving monotonically: 16 dips below 8's mean, 32 is the single highest point measured, and 50, the largest budget, comes in below 32. The per-budget ranges widen at the same point, run to run variation at labels_per_class=50 spans 0.598 to 0.714, a wider spread than the entire climb from 0 to 8 labels. Three nested-sampling runs isn't enough to average out that much variance, and I wouldn't claim the curve has a true peak at 32 from this data, only that recovery is fast and reliable in the low-data regime and noisy without a clear further trend once a few dozen labels per category are already in hand.

Saliency failed on its first real run, a bug the unit tests couldn't have caught since none of them exercise a real trained model: `self.gradients` was `None` inside `GradCAM.compute`, an `AttributeError` on the very first Grad-CAM call. The cause: the classifier head training deliberately freezes the backbone (`requires_grad=False` on every convolutional weight, the locked protocol's `supervised_trainable_part: classifier_head`), and with no tensor anywhere in the input requiring grad either, PyTorch's autograd never builds a graph through the frozen convolutional layers at all, so the backward hook on `conv_1x1` had nothing to fire on. Fixed by explicitly calling `.requires_grad_(True)` on a clone of the input pixel tensor before the forward pass in `GradCAM.compute`: PyTorch marks an operation's output as requiring grad if any of its inputs does, regardless of whether the operation's own parameters are frozen, so this restores a differentiable path through the backbone for computing input and activation gradients without touching which weights actually receive an optimizer step. Reran after the fix and it worked on the first attempt.

Also caught, on inspecting the first successful saliency run's output before trusting it: all 12 saved overlays were of the same true category, `calculator`. `shifted_test.json` is grouped by category, and taking the first 12 misses unfiltered over that manifest landed entirely inside whichever category sorts first, saying nothing about any other category's failure modes. Changed the selection to one miss per true category, up to 12 categories, and reran; the resulting 12 overlays now span 12 different true categories. Looked at one of them directly, a real "computer" (a desktop tower) misclassified as "laptop": the CAM heat concentrates on the case's front panel and drive bay, a real region of the actual object rather than scattered over the background, which is the check that matters here, since a saliency map that looked uniform or background-focused would have meant the earlier gradient fix produced a mathematically defined but meaningless map rather than a genuinely informative one.
